StericX physical-organic featurization engine · Rust

Independent reproduction study

Reproducing a published quantum-chemistry descriptor from scratch — and finding exactly where it drifts.

StericX is a ground-up reimplementation of Sterimol and buried-volume ligand descriptors. What it reproduces to reference precision, the two published reaction models it carries, the single binary that runs far faster than the reference — and, in full view, what it doesn't.

0.9986 R² against the published Kraken buried-volume descriptor, computed on the authors' own DFT geometries (Pearson r = 0.9998, 11 ligands).

The experiment

One variable changed per step

The native descriptor fell short of the published values in earlier studies. Rather than guess why, each step below changes exactly one thing — geometry source, then coordination-centre convention — and re-measures agreement with the published vbur_max_delta_qvbur_min.

RDKit / MMFF geometry · force field 0.8626
CREST / GFN2-xTB geometry · semi-empirical 0.9254
Kraken's DFT geometry · 2.1 Å centre 0.9937
+ Kraken's 2.28 Å convention · documented value 0.9986

Bar length maps R² over a 0.80–1.00 axis. Perfect agreement = full width.

Swapping the force-field geometry for Kraken's own DFT structures — and nothing else — lifted agreement from 0.86 to 0.99, proving the kernel was never the problem. The last remaining offset traced to a single number: Kraken's descriptor code places the reference metal 2.28 Å from phosphorus, not the 2.1 Å used to isolate the geometry. Adopting their documented value closed it.

The diverse-set R² sits just below the eleven-ligand figure — expected across such varied chemistry — while the typical ligand matches to a tenth of a cubic ångström. That number, not a cherry-picked best case, is the honest headline.

Scaling to the full library also surfaced a real kernel bug the eleven trisubstituted ligands could never trigger: the quadrant frame identified a donor's substituents as its three nearest heavy atoms, which silently mis-framed primary and secondary phosphines (R–PH₂, R₂P–H) by discarding their bonded hydrogens. Selecting the covalently bonded atoms instead — hydrogens included — removed every spurious zero and lifted R² from 0.9649 to 0.9852 without dropping a single ligand (the validated count in fact rose). The residual is heavy-tailed: median error 0.11 Å³, 90th percentile 0.71 Å³, and R² rises to 0.9897 once the thinnest 1% tail is set aside.

And it holds for a second, independent steric descriptor. Kraken measures Sterimol along the coordination axis — the same 2.28 Å metal centre the buried volume uses — a convention recovered, not assumed: 2.28 Å is the one distance at which the published lengths fall on the diagonal. With that matched, StericX reproduces Kraken's published Sterimol L, B₁, and B₅ across all 1,541 ligands, mean R² = 0.9887:

A third descriptor class closes even tighter. Kraken publishes two geometric pyramidalization descriptors for the donor — pyr_P (Radhakrishnan) and pyr_alpha (mean out-of-plane angle). Both reduce to closed forms on the donor's three bond vectors, verified against morfeus to machine precision before implementation. Because they depend only on those three directions — not on the metal centre, sphere, or lone-pair conventions that bound the buried volume — the native kernel reproduces the published values almost exactly, mean R² = 0.99998:

Beyond the descriptors

It carries a second, independent reaction model

A descriptor is only as useful as the chemistry it explains. StericX's buried volume was put to work on a different published study — the Newman-Stonebraker %Vbur(min) classifier that sorts phosphines as active or inactive across a family of Ni cross-coupling reactions.

StericX recomputes the exact descriptor the model is built on (R² = 0.9992 across 479 ligand–reaction points), and a single-node threshold on it recovers the paper's own classifier — the same ~32% decision boundary, direction, and accuracy/MCC. Pushed further, one universal threshold pooled across six Ni reactions holds out-of-sample; and re-run on the authors' own DFT geometries — no shared coordinates — the descriptor still lands at R² = 0.9735 against the published values. The classifier's MCC is deliberately moderate — it is a one-number steric model — and that limit is reported alongside the match.

Across 267 ligand–reaction points StericX's %Vbur(min) matches the published value at R² = 0.9994, and its single-node classifier independently recovers the "bulky-active" direction for every one of the six Pd reactions — the mirror image of the Ni cliff. Two of the six are lifted from other groups' published work (Zhao, Science 2018; Stambuli et al.), a genuine off-bench test that reproduces cleanly. And the honesty holds in the other direction too: Reaction VII, which the paper's own SI flags as pathological, is exactly where StericX's independent fit also disagrees, and the weak Heck dataset is weak for everyone — reported, not dropped.

Why a practitioner would use it

The same numbers, ≈14× faster

Fidelity is half the value; the other half is cost. Computing the identical buried-volume descriptor over the whole 1,546-ligand library, on one CPU core, timed end-to-end against morfeus — the reference Python implementation.

morfeus · Python + scientific stack 81 / s
StericX · single Rust binary 1,110 / s

Bar length maps single-core throughput. Conservative: StericX also computes Sterimol and pyramidalization in the same timed pass.

Not a cheaper computation: of the 1,534 phosphines morfeus can frame, 1,518 agree to R² = 0.999999 (the 16 that differ are a documented frame-convention edge, not error). StericX ships as one 1.9 MB dependency-free binary — no interpreter, no conda, no morfeus install — so the speedup arrives with zero deployment surface.

A prediction on the record

A frozen, falsifiable forecast

The strongest test of a model is one it can fail. Ten ligands outside the training set are frozen with predicted selectivities, cryptographically hashed and dated before any measurement.

Every prediction carries a 95% interval and a leverage-based applicability-domain judgement, and the document commits to an exact experimental protocol and a pre-registered pass/fail rule — measured ΔΔG‡ within the interval for at least six of eight primary candidates and the correct ordering, else falsified. It states its own limit plainly: for 7 of 10 candidates the interval spans both enantiomers, so those near-racemate predictions are not falsifiable on selectivity, and the test runs on ΔΔG‡ instead. Unmeasured by design — the rigor of the open prediction is the point.

What it does not claim

  • Compact descriptors don't replace the published one. StericX's own feature set does not reproduce the ten-ligand Ni-catalyzed homo-Diels–Alder selectivity model (native leave-one-out Q² ≈ 0.00) — a deliberate ablation, reported not hidden.
  • Better geometry didn't improve the kinetic model. Higher descriptor fidelity did not raise held-out Q² on that small n = 11 family; the two are decoupled here.
  • A residual tail remains — and is understood. On the full set the median error is 0.11 Å³ (90th percentile 0.71 Å³). Classifying by donor shows the 1,517 tertiary phosphines (98.4%) are unbiased, while the entire bias sits in 24 primary/secondary phosphines and grows ~0.7 Å³ per P–H bond — the signature of the geometric lone-pair centre standing in for Kraken's exact xTB centre, not a bug and not tuned away.
  • No measured prospective validation — but a falsifiable one is on the record. The ten-candidate deck is frozen as a hash-anchored pre-registration with per-ligand uncertainty and an explicit falsification protocol; its experimental outcomes are unmeasured, so no predictive-success claim is made. A lab measurement is what would close it.

Method & attribution

Kraken's DFT geometries were retrieved from the public MolSSI descriptor-library REST API; the API's descriptor values match the published dataset, confirming provenance. Buried volumes use a deterministic voxel grid at Kraken's convention (3.5 Å sphere, Bondi radii ×1.17, 2.28 Å metal distance).
